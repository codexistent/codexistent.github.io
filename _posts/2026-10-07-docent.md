#  Docent

Recently, through ~~sitting down and interviewing~~ talking with Benjamin Qi, I was made aware of the [OpenAI-HuggingFace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI%E2%80%93HuggingFace_incident) incident. While being no surprise to me given many other recent questionable happenings with AI models, this one particularly caught my eye due to its *initiative*: instead of being triggered by human (such as the Naiver-Stokes OpenAI incident), the agent itself had escaped from its sandbox, thereafter reaching out and in fact collaborating with *other* agents to cause havoc.

Amidst ongoing developments, AI safety and alignment is without question a critical field. So when reading Transluce AI's post, "Introducing Docent", a particular quote caught my eye which I felt particularly echoed my own thoughts on the subject:
> We envision a future where model understanding is rich and counterfactual by default, and scales using AI tools.

I interpret this as enabling AI tools which can better understand AI itself, adjacent to or at a rate beyond the pace of AI development, and believe this is possibly the only way--if not mighty difficult of one--to ensure safety in the coming future. However, a lingering question of mine (with undertones of worry, due to my strong hope for a solid, sustainable framework monetary-wise for the AI safety industry) was, what were these companies'--or the AI safety industry's, for that matter--revenue models, such as to amply support continuing research? And in addition, could they be further improved for even better organic backing?

In light of this, today, I tried Docent: Transluce AI's tool for monitoring, improving scaffolding of, and detecting surprising behaviors on AI models. 

## The Tool
I set up Docent with one of my recent GitHub projects, 'leep-dearning' (it hosts demos of a few of my deep learning models), by running a few terminal commands, following the documentation's instructions. Then, I ran a Claude Code session on my application and `/export`ed it, placing the resulting file within a sub-folder of my project (although it doesn't quite matter ofcourse where you place it). Within a couple more terminal commands, I was able to generate a collection (consisting of a breakdown of the blocks generated by the user, assistant, and tools) for this exported file which appeared on my dashboard on [docent.transluce.org](docent.transluce.org), ready for analysis. 

I was now able to (via the chat interface within `Docent dashboard` > `Runs` > **Clicking on my collection**) simply use natural language prompts to discuss points of failure, mistakes, or surprising behaviors the collection may have had. My first chat output/result was a list of around 8 of the largest issues which Docent had detected in my collection, including:
- User prompt issues (or how they could be improved)
- Tool-Assistant interaction inconsistencies ~ such as incidents where the Tool never successfully returned what the Assistant had queried for, leading the Assistant to declare some unsupported statement
- Hallucinations where the Assistant claims to have but failed to perform certain actions
- Performance claims not supported by measurements

One of the key features I enjoyed about this tool were the inline quotations in the chat outputs, which if I clicked, would pull me to the place in the collection that was being referenced. An areas that I could see improvement in was some parts of documentation (there were a few small hiccups I ran into along the way when trying out the tool), although this would surely improve with time and increase in usage.

At this point, it felt interesting to see an AI model evaluating another model's/agent's outputs, which created a think-yourself-into-a-hole moment for me: if we had AI models, and we always tried to evaluate them, such as to evaluate the evaluation models, and so forth, would we ever truly be able to fully evaluate the intentions of all these models? How could we be sure that we weren't just being deceived by a non-aligned stack of donuts, only to find all of them with holes in the middle? Perhaps this is thinking too far, but in any case it was interesting to think about, and to create a metaphor for.

Therefore, this lead me to another possible area/feature regarding the tool: that it could have 'trust ratings' on each of its own judgements. Ofcourse, this ties into benchmarking and iteratively improving the tool itself, but I think having a visual anchor confirming that, indeed, at the end of the day AI is AI would somehow seem to be another stamp of trustworthiness for me. Alas, these are only my own two cents.

## Conclusion
While this was only, among a few, another start to my explorations into AI safety tools, there is still much to encompass even for this tool alone, and it feels like I have just touched the surface. I am also interested in seeing how Docent's backend/code itself works, as it is open source.

Now back to my original question: what were these AI safety and alignment companies' revenue models? After experiencing Docent, I was surprised to later find Transluce AI to be non-profit, and Docent to be open source. So I guess for now, we'll never know. Importantly, my key findings from all this were as follows:
1. Donate.
2. Not all non-profits are doo doo.
